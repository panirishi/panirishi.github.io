Section I

The Argument

Why does Silicon Valley sound like the Old Testament?

In the spring of 2023, Marc Andreessen, one of Silicon Valley's most influential venture capitalists, published what he called a "Techno-Optimist Manifesto." Written in the cadence of biblical prophetic literature and concluding with a curated list of intellectual "saints," the document declared that technology is "the glory of human ambition and achievement" — and that its authors were there to "bring the good news." The religious register was not incidental. It was the point.

This project argues that the figure of Moses — prophet, liberator, lawgiver, and shepherd — provides the most illuminating framework for understanding how Silicon Valley's tech leaders construct, perform, and legitimize that authority. Moses is rarely invoked by name in contemporary tech discourse. But the structural logic of the Exodus narrative runs through it like an undercurrent: a visionary leader perceives a truth that others cannot yet see; he names the enemy (inefficiency, ignorance, mortality, planetary extinction); he leads a people out of bondage and through a wilderness of disruption; and he orients them, always, toward a promised land just beyond the horizon.

"He seems to see himself as a Moses, someone who makes it possible to pass through the wilderness — but never sets foot in the Promised Land."

— Aeon, on Elon Musk and his mission to colonize Mars

This is the story Elon Musk tells about Mars. It is the story Mark Zuckerberg tells about global connection. It is the story the AI industry tells about the future of human intelligence itself. To make this argument, I draw on Michael Walzer's foundational study Exodus and Revolution (1985), which traces how the Exodus narrative has historically served as the template for Western political and revolutionary thought — from the Puritan settlers to the abolitionists to Marxist revolutionaries. That structure, I argue, has migrated into the secular mythology of Silicon Valley, where it does the same cultural work it has always done: lending moral weight to leadership, urgency to mission, and a sense of collective destiny to what might otherwise look like the self-interest of the very powerful.

Section II

Moses & the Exodus Narrative

A structural figure, not merely a theological one

To understand why the Mosaic archetype resonates so powerfully in contemporary tech discourse, we must first understand what makes Moses such a durable political and cultural symbol. Moses is not, as political philosopher Michael Walzer argues in Exodus and Revolution, primarily a theological figure. He is a structural one. The Exodus narrative follows a precise sequence — oppression, revelation, liberation, wilderness, covenant, promised land — and it is this sequence that has made the story endlessly adaptable across centuries and contexts.

Moses himself occupies a peculiar position within this structure. He is not the promised land's inheritor — he never sets foot in it. He is the figure who perceives the destination, names the enemy, absorbs the people's doubt and rage, and keeps them moving. His authority rests not on institutional power but on a claim to divine revelation — moral before it is political, prophetic before it is managerial.
